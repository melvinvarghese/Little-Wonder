# The Curious Past — source verification notes

Research links were opened and read directly (not used from search snippets alone). These notes identify the evidence used for each article and flag where an interpretation needs qualification.

1. **A Shipwrecked Machine That Mapped the Sky — Antikythera mechanism**
   - Smithsonian Magazine, [“Decoding the Antikythera Mechanism, the First Computer”](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/decoding-antikythera-mechanism-first-computer-180953979/)
   - Supports: recovery of bronze fragments from the wreck in 1900–01; X-ray/CT study revealing gears and inscriptions; inferred dials for celestial cycles, calendars, and eclipses. The article notes missing components and active scholarly debate. The article avoids asserting a settled maker or exact date.

2. **The Inventor Behind the Flat-Bottomed Paper Bag — Margaret Knight**
   - Smithsonian American Women's History Museum, [“Margaret Knight Invented a Machine that Shapes the Way We Shop”](https://womenshistory.si.edu/blog/margaret-knight-invented-machine-shapes-way-we-shop)
   - Supports: Knight's machine mechanized flat-bottom paper-bag production; her prototype and patent dispute with Charles Annan; her evidence and the patent decision; her wider record of inventions and patents. Smithsonian collection metadata independently confirms that her 1879 model improved on patent 116,842, granted in 1871. Additional object record: [Patent Model for Paper Bag Machine](https://www.si.edu/object/patent-model-paper-bag-machine%3Anmah_214303).

3. **The Fossil Hunter Who Changed the Prehistoric World — Mary Anning**
   - Natural History Museum, London, [“Mary Anning: The unsung hero of fossil discovery”](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/mary-anning-unsung-hero.html)
   - Supports: Anning's Lyme Regis fossil work; her excavation of the ichthyosaur skeleton found initially by her brother; discovery of a complete plesiosaur and pterosaur remains; exclusion from scientific societies and frequent lack of attribution; her self-directed learning.

4. **Ada Lovelace Saw Beyond the Numbers — Analytical Engine**
   - Science Museum, [“Charles Babbage’s Difference Engines and the Science Museum”](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects-and-stories/charles-babbages-difference-engines-and-science-museum)
   - Supports: Babbage's Analytical Engine was never completed in his lifetime; Lovelace published her account in 1843; her notes described a general-purpose mechanical device, punched-card programmability, and the possibility of manipulating symbols beyond numerical calculations. The article avoids claiming the engine actually ran or that Lovelace's speculative vision was an implemented computer. Bodleian Libraries’ account says the Bernoulli-number table is often called the first program, while clarifying it is more accurately an execution trace than the punched-card program itself: [“Ada Lovelace and the Analytical Engine”](https://blogs.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/adalovelace/2018/07/26/ada-lovelace-and-the-analytical-engine/).

5. **Alice Ball's Breakthrough—and the Credit She Nearly Lost — Alice Ball**
   - University of Hawai‘i Foundation, [“A Woman Who Changed the World”](https://uhfoundation.org/impact/students/woman-who-changed-world)
   - Supports: Ball's injectable chaulmoogra-oil treatment; its historical importance until later medicines appeared in the 1940s; her death before publication and later recovery of credit through Harry Hollmann; her master's degree at the College of Hawai‘i. Article explicitly does not describe the method as a cure.

6. **The White Flecks That May Help Roman Concrete Mend — Roman concrete**
   - MIT News, [“Riddle solved: Why was Roman concrete so durable?”](https://news.mit.edu/2023/roman-concrete-durability-lime-casts-0106)
   - Supports: 2023 researchers' hot-mixing hypothesis, the lime clasts observed in Privernum samples, and the team's laboratory crack-healing tests. Article properly frames this as a study-specific proposal and not a universal explanation for all Roman concrete or structures.

7. **An Ancient Temple Had a Coin-Operated Dispenser — Heron of Alexandria**
   - Smith College History of Science Museum exhibit, [“Coin-Operated Holy Water Dispensing Machine”](https://www.smith.edu/bigbad/historyscitech/museum/ancient_inventions/hsc18b.htm)
   - Supports: attribution of a temple holy-water dispenser to Heron and the coin-weight/pan/valve mechanism. Article distinguishes the described design from evidence of a surviving machine and uses “vending machine” as a modern analogy, not a literal claim about snack sales.

8. **London's First Traffic Light Ran on Gas — 1868 signal**
   - Washington State Magazine (Washington State University), [“Traffic signals: A brief history”](https://magazine.wsu.edu/web-extra/traffic-signals-a-brief-history/)
   - Supports: gas-lit signal at the Houses of Parliament in December 1868; J. P. Knight's proposal; semaphore arms by day, red/green gas lights at night, and manual police operation; reported explosions and injuries. The article distinguishes this experiment from later electric traffic signals.

9. **The Engineer Who Sent London's Sewage Underground — Joseph Bazalgette**
   - London Museum, [“How Bazalgette built London's first super-sewer”](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/how-bazalgette-built-londons-first-super-sewer/)
   - Supports: Great Stink context; Bazalgette's role in the main drainage system; approximately 1,100 miles of drains and 82 miles of major sewers; continuing use of parts of the network; later need for treatment and modern upgrades. Article avoids saying Bazalgette alone invented sanitation or that his system immediately stopped all river pollution.

10. **The Botanist Who Made a Book of Blue Seaweed — Anna Atkins**
    - New York Public Library, [“Anna Atkins's Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions”](https://www.nypl.org/events/exhibitions/galleries/visual-world/item/4105)
    - Natural History Museum, London, [“Anna Atkins’s cyanotypes: The first book of photographs”](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/anna-atkins-cyanotypes-the-first-book-of-photographs.html)
    - Supports: Atkins's 1843 cyanotype plates of algae; the process and use for botanical specimens; her publication in parts and recognition as the first photographically illustrated book. NYPL says she made images of more than 400 specimens; NHM describes the first book and cyanotype process.
