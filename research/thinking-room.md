# The Thinking Room — Source verification notes

All sources below are Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entries. Each page was opened and read for the evidence summarized here; article text uses these as factual grounding, not as authority for a single settled answer. The featured verdicts are explicitly presented as disputed where appropriate.

1. **The Ship That Kept Its Name** — [Identity Over Time](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-time/). SEP describes the classic case as one ship gradually having all its planks replaced (Replacement), while the removed original planks are reassembled into another ship (Reassembly). If each seems a candidate for being the original, transitivity of identity creates the puzzle. Competing accounts include constitution and strict/loose identity; no solution is asserted as settled.
2. **Would You Plug In?** — [Well-Being](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/well-being/). SEP presents Nozick’s machine as offering a lifetime of chosen experiences while disconnecting the user from ordinary reality, and explains its use as an objection to hedonism about well-being. It also notes responses and warns against treating the thought experiment as a decisive refutation.
3. **The Switch and the Push** — [The Doctrine of Double Effect](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/double-effect/). SEP contrasts diverting a trolley from five people onto a track with one person against pushing a bystander onto the track. It explains how some use intended means versus foreseen side effects to account for different judgments, while noting disagreement about both the principle and the cases; foreseeing harm alone does not establish permissibility.
4. **Are Your Memories You?** — [Personal Identity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/). SEP covers memory and broader psychological-continuity accounts, and reports objections to a simple memory criterion: memory continuity can fail transitivity, and identifying genuine memory may presuppose identity. Psychological continuity and its alternatives remain disputed.
5. **The Rules Behind the Veil** — [The Original Position](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/original-position/). SEP explains that Rawls’s hypothetical parties lack particular facts such as class, wealth, race, gender, abilities, and personal conception of the good, but retain general knowledge about people and society. This is a device for impartial choice, not literal ignorance; its implications are debated.
6. **The Lucky Clock** — [The Analysis of Knowledge](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-analysis/). SEP presents stopped-clock and other cases as counterexamples to the idea that justified true belief is sufficient for knowledge. In a stopped-clock case, a belief can be true and supported by apparently normal perception while being true by luck. Philosophers disagree about the missing condition or best analysis.
7. **Two Prisoners, One Bad Outcome** — [Prisoner’s Dilemma](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/prisoner-dilemma/). SEP’s standard symmetric 2×2 setup has defection strictly dominate cooperation for each player under the stipulated payoff ordering; mutual defection is the sole strict Nash equilibrium, although both players prefer mutual cooperation. This is a model with defined incentives, not a claim that all real interactions have that structure.
8. **How Do You Know I Feel?** — [Other Minds](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/other-minds/). SEP distinguishes the everyday question of how to understand another person’s feelings from the radical skeptical question of whether others have minds at all. Proposed approaches include analogy, inference to the best explanation, testimony, criteria, and perceptual knowledge; there is no consensus that one answer resolves every version.
9. **One Grain at a Time** — [Vagueness](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/vagueness/). SEP explains the sorites structure: a plausible starting point plus an apparently harmless one-step rule can yield an absurd conclusion, as with a heap or child. The challenge concerns vague predicates and borderline cases; theories such as contextualism are among the responses, not a settled fix.
10. **The Predictor’s Two Boxes** — [Causal Decision Theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/decision-causal/). SEP sets out Newcomb’s problem: the transparent box contains $1,000, the opaque box contains either $0 or $1,000,000 based on a reliable earlier prediction. Taking both dominates one-boxing for each fixed prediction, while expected-utility reasoning that conditions on the reliable prediction favors one-boxing. The conflict between principles and recommended choice is a live dispute.
